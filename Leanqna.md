Block 1 — The Kernel & Core Type Theory (6)

Q1. What exactly does the Lean kernel check, and why can a small kernel justify trusting a huge system? 
The kernel is a small C++ type checker for a dependent type theory (a variant of the Calculus of Inductive Constructions). It knows: dependent function types, universes Sort u, inductive families with recursors, a primitive quotient construction, and definitional equality (β, δ, ζ, η, ι-reduction, proof irrelevance, structure η), with fast paths for Nat and string literals. The elaborator, tactics (simp, grind, omega), macros, and notation are all untrusted programs whose only output is a term. The kernel re-checks that term from scratch. This is the de Bruijn criterion: trust is concentrated in a tiny checker, while the large, buggy, ever-changing automation only has to produce certificates the checker accepts. A bug in grind yields a failed proof, not a false theorem.

Q2. What does it mean that Prop is impredicative and proof-irrelevant, and what does each property buy and cost? 
Prop is Sort 0. Impredicativity: a ∀ over any type, even a huge universe, still lands in Prop (because imax u 0 = 0). That makes ∀ P : Prop, P → P a proposition and lets you quantify over all propositions. Proof irrelevance: any two proofs p q : P with P : Prop are definitionally equal, so the kernel never compares proof terms. This keeps type checking fast, lets subtypes behave well, and keeps the cost of a proof from leaking into computation. The cost is that data can't be freely extracted from proofs (see Q8), and some metatheory gets subtle (known wrinkles, such as subject reduction around Acc, are documented but have not been exploited to derive False).

Q3. Why does Lean need imax in addition to max for universe levels, and why aren't universes cumulative? A function type (x : α) → β x with α : Sort u and β x : Sort v lives in Sort (imax u v), where imax u 0 = 0 and otherwise imax u v = max u v. The special case is what keeps "functions into Prop" inside Prop (impredicativity). Plain max would push them up a level. Lean's universes are not cumulative: Type u is not a subtype of Type (u+1). A value must be explicitly lifted with ULift/PLift. The payoff is simpler, more predictable unification, because every term has one universe rather than a family of subsumption-related ones. The price is occasional manual lifting in universe-heavy category theory.

Q4. Distinguish definitional equality from propositional equality, and say precisely what the kernel decides. Propositional equality is the inductive type Eq a b : Prop, which you must prove. Definitional equality (a ≡ b) is a judgment the kernel decides by reduction. It normalizes both sides with weak-head reduction and lazy δ-unfolding (guided by definitional height), applies η for functions and structures, applies proof irrelevance, and uses GMP-backed Nat literal arithmetic. If two terms are defeq, rfl : a = b type-checks with no further proof. This is why by decide or rfl can "prove" 2^10 = 1024 instantly, and why a careless rfl over a large unfolding can hang the kernel. The elaborator has its own transparency settings (Q18), but the kernel always unfolds everything.

Q5. How does Lean get function extensionality without postulating it? Lean builds in a quotient primitive: Quot r, Quot.mk, Quot.lift, Quot.ind, plus a kernel reduction rule Quot.lift f h (Quot.mk r a) ≡ f a. The only quotient-related axiom is Quot.sound : r a b → Quot.mk r a = Quot.mk r b. From these, funext is derivable (the classical Quot-based argument: quotient functions by pointwise equality and use lift to extract the equation). Lean's three standard axioms are propext, Classical.choice, and Quot.sound. #print axioms showing only these three is the usual definition of "a clean classical proof."

Q6. What is known about Lean's consistency, and where does the metatheory stand? On paper, the type theory is argued consistent relative to ZFC plus a countable hierarchy of inaccessible cardinals (Mario Carneiro's The Type Theory of Lean), and lean4lean reimplements the kernel in Lean as a step toward verifying the checker itself. Real-world kernel soundness bugs have been found and fixed over the years, but none has been known to be exploited in a deployed formalization. The honest framing is that the kernel is empirically trustworthy and partially formally verified. That gap is exactly why independent re-checkers exist (Q29). [2026] The Lean FRO's timeline lists con-leche, an external checker "proven in Lean to be consistent," continuing that line of work.
Block 2 — Inductive Types, Recursion, Termination (6)

Q7. What does an inductive declaration generate, and why is everything else reducible to it? Declaring inductive Nat | zero | succ (n : Nat) makes the kernel generate the type, constructors, and a recursor Nat.rec : {motive : Nat → Sort u} → motive zero → ((n : Nat) → motive n → motive (succ n)) → (t : Nat) → motive t, with ι-reduction rules computing it on constructors. Pattern matching (match), structural recursion (brecOn/below), and the induction tactic all elaborate down to recursors. Declarations are rejected if they violate strict positivity (the type may not appear to the left of an arrow in a constructor argument), since otherwise you could encode a fixed-point combinator and derive False.

Q8. Why can an inductive predicate in Prop usually only eliminate into Prop — and what are the exceptions? If Or p q : Prop could eliminate into Type, you could define a function Or p p → Bool returning different values for the "left" and "right" proof. But proof irrelevance says all proofs are defeq, so the two results would be forced equal, an inconsistency. So Prop-inductives get large elimination only if they're syntactic subsingletons: at most one constructor, with every field either a Prop or determined by the indices. Eq, And, True, False, and Acc qualify. That is why you can use Eq.rec to transport data along an equality (needed for dependent types), but cannot case on Or to compute a number.

Q9. Contrast structural and well-founded recursion in how they compute and in what you can do with them. Structural recursion compiles to brecOn over the recursor, so it reduces by kernel computation: rfl and decide can evaluate it. Well-founded recursion (termination_by / decreasing_by) is defined with WellFounded.fix over an Acc predicate. The definition is not a closed-form recursor application. It unfolds only via a proved fixpoint equation, so Lean marks such definitions irreducible by default (since 2024). For proofs, use the auto-generated equation lemmas (unfold, rw [f], or simp [f]) rather than rfl. The design trades definitional computation for the ability to write natural algorithms (Euclid's GCD, merge sort) whose termination isn't syntactic.

Q10. What are partial and unsafe, and why can't proofs depend on them? partial def f requires the return type to be Inhabited. The kernel sees an opaque constant whose only known property is inhabitation, while the compiler emits the real recursive code via implemented_by. So you cannot prove anything about its behaviour, and non-termination can't leak into logic. unsafe additionally bypasses positivity, termination, and some type discipline (you can use unsafeCast) and cannot be referenced from safe definitions or theorems. This is the mechanism by which Lean is simultaneously a general-purpose language (loops, FFI, mutation tricks) and a sound logic.

Q11. Why is structure η definitional, and why does it matter for the algebra library? For a structure S, the kernel treats ⟨x.1, x.2⟩ ≡ x as definitional (and projections are primitive Expr.proj terms). Consequences: unification can solve ?m =?= ⟨a, b⟩ by projections, anonymous-constructor rewrites are lossless, and instance diamonds in a class hierarchy (e.g., two paths from Ring to AddMonoid) can be made to agree by definitional unfolding at instance transparency rather than by a propositional lemma. Mathlib's hierarchy design (Q37) depends on this: a diamond that is only propositionally equal breaks rw/simp in subtle ways.

Q12. How does Lean support nested and mutual inductives and indexed families? The kernel natively supports mutual inductives and indexed families (Vec α n), where a constructor may refine indices. Nested inductives (inductive Tree | node : List Tree → Tree) are translated to an equivalent mutual declaration with an auxiliary type, then the recursors are translated back. Indexed families complicate cases/match: the tactic must unify the scrutinee's indices with each constructor's (using injectivity and noConfusion), and it fails when indices are not variables unless you generalize them first. That is the root cause of most "dependent elimination failed" errors.
Block 3 — Elaboration, Unification, Typeclasses (6)

Q13. What is "elaboration" and why is it more than type checking? The elaborator turns surface syntax into kernel terms: it fills implicit arguments, resolves overloads and coercions, runs typeclass resolution, and elaborates tactic blocks. It is bidirectional (expected types flow in, inferred types flow out) and creates metavariables for unknowns. It postpones problems it can't yet solve (e.g., a numeral whose type isn't determined) and resumes them when more information arrives, applying default instances as a last resort. Errors like "typeclass instance problem is stuck" mean metavariables remained unassigned after postponed problems ran out. The kernel then re-checks the finished term, so elaborator heuristics never need to be trusted.

Q14. What does isDefEq (the elaborator's unifier) actually do, and why is it incomplete? It decides whether two terms can be made definitionally equal, assigning metavariables along the way. It handles first-order unification, Miller higher-order patterns (?m x y =?= t where x, y are distinct bound variables), η, structure η, lazy δ-unfolding, and approximations (e.g., first-order approximation of ?f a =?= g b). Higher-order unification is undecidable in general, so Lean makes heuristic choices: it can fail on solvable problems or commit to a non-principal solution. This is why elaboration order and explicit type ascriptions sometimes change whether a term works.

Q15. How does typeclass resolution work, and what causes its performance cliffs? Lean uses tabled resolution: a Prolog-style backward chaining search with memoization (tabling) of subgoals, so repeated subgoals are solved once and cyclic instance graphs terminate. Instances have priorities, outParam arguments mark inputs that are determined by the search, and default_instance resolves leftover ambiguity. Performance cliffs come from deep hierarchies where one goal fans out into many paths (Mathlib's algebraic classes), from unification at high transparency inside instance checks, and from non-reducible diamonds. Much of Mathlib's performance engineering is typeclass engineering.

Q16. How do coercions work, and why do norm_cast/push_cast need @[coe]? When types mismatch, the elaborator inserts a coercion by solving CoeT α a β via instance chaining, then unfolds the coercion instance so the term contains the real function (e.g., Nat.cast) rather than an opaque Coe.coe. Functions tagged @[coe] are displayed as ↑ and recognized by norm_cast, which classifies cast lemmas as elim, move, or squash and uses them to push casts to the leaves and cancel them. Without the attribute, a cast is just an anonymous function application and the tactic can't reason about it.

Q17. What does match compile to? Each match becomes an auxiliary matcher definition (foo.match_1) built from casesOn/recursor applications, with an equation lemma per arm generated on demand. For dependent matches, the elaborator generalizes indices and discriminants so the motive type-checks. Overlapping patterns are resolved top-down, and the compiler proves exhaustiveness (missing cases are errors). Because the matcher is a regular definition, simp/split/rw can reason about it through its equation lemmas rather than unfolding to recursors.

Q18. Explain transparency levels and why simp cannot "see through" a definition that rfl can. The elaborator's whnf/isDefEq run under a transparency mode: reducible (only @[reducible] defs), instances (plus instances), default (plus ordinary defs, but not @[irreducible] ones), and all. simp and rw match patterns at reducible/instances for speed and predictability; rfl and exact run at default. So def double n := n + n isn't unfolded by simp unless you pass it as a simp lemma, even though rfl can prove double 2 = 4. Marking things @[reducible] or @[irreducible] is how library authors control both what proofs break and how fast unification runs. The kernel ignores all of this and unfolds everything.
Block 4 — Tactics, Metaprogramming, Automation (7)

Q19. What is a tactic, mechanically? A goal is a metavariable with a local context and a type. A tactic is a monadic program (TacticM) that assigns a goal's metavariable to a term, possibly containing new metavariables (the new goals). A finished proof is the tree of those assignments, instantiated into one term for the kernel. So intro, apply, cases, and simp are all "term builders" with different strategies. sorry assigns sorryAx, which taints the theorem and is visible in #print axioms.

Q20. Why does rw sometimes fail with "motive is not type correct"? rw [h : a = b] abstracts occurrences of a in the goal into a lambda motive := fun x => ... (via kabstract) and applies Eq.mpr/congrArg. If some other term in the goal's type depends on a (a proof or a dependent type mentioning it), replacing only some occurrences makes the lambda ill-typed. Fixes: simp only, generalize first, use subst when you have a variable equation, or restructure with cast/HEq. It is a direct consequence of dependent types: rewriting is only free when nothing depends on the rewritten term.

Q21. How does simp work and why does it terminate? simp uses a discrimination tree index to find candidate rewrite rules (@[simp] lemmas, simprocs, hypotheses), rewrites subterms bottom-up using congruence lemmas so it knows which argument positions it may rewrite, and records each rewrite as an Eq proof composed with congrArg/Eq.trans. Termination is not guaranteed in general, so the community discipline is that simp lemmas should orient toward a normal form. For permutation lemmas (e.g., commutativity) it uses ordered rewriting, applying a rule only if it makes the term smaller in a term order, to avoid looping. simp? prints the exact lemma list, which is the standard way to turn a fragile simp into a stable simp only [...].

Q22. How does omega decide linear arithmetic over Nat and Int, and what is incomplete about it? omega normalizes hypotheses and goal into linear (in)equalities over Int (casting Nat and adding nonnegativity facts), handling / and % by literals through introduced variables. It then runs a Fourier–Motzkin-style elimination with exact integer reasoning (equalities solved by the omega-test's method, bounds tightened by GCD normalization), and emits a proof term checked by the kernel. The documentation notes the dark and grey shadow steps of the full Omega algorithm are not implemented, so it is incomplete for some satisfiable-over-rationals-but-not-integers cases. When it fails you may need case splits.

Q23. What is grind, and how is it different from simp? [2026] grind (shipped in Lean 4.22, 2025, and now a headline feature) is an SMT-style engine inside the tactic framework. It maintains an e-graph with congruence closure, instantiates quantified lemmas via E-matching, performs case splitting, and calls theory solvers (linear integer arithmetic, commutative rings, and others). Where simp rewrites a goal toward normal form, grind accumulates facts about a whole context and closes goals by contradiction, with every step producing a kernel-checkable proof term. It is why the lean-lang.org front-page examples prove things like n! + 1 having a large prime factor in a handful of grind calls.

Q24. How does bv_decide reach SAT-solver power while staying checkable? It bit-blasts a bit-vector goal to a boolean circuit (CNF), runs an external SAT solver (CaDiCaL), and receives an LRAT unsatisfiability certificate. A verified checker for LRAT, written in Lean, validates it. But: the verified checker is executed by the compiler, and the resulting equality is justified through the axiom Lean.ofReduceBool. So bv_decide proofs trust the Lean compiler/evaluator in addition to the kernel (see Q27). That is a deliberate performance-versus-trust trade, and #print axioms will show it.

Q25. Explain macro hygiene and why Lean's syntax is extensible. Parsing is table-driven: syntax categories (term, tactic, command) are extended by syntax/notation/macro declarations that add Pratt-parser entries at runtime. Macros are functions Syntax → MacroM Syntax. Hygiene gives every identifier introduced by a macro a fresh macro scope, so a macro's internal names can't capture or be captured by the user's variables. Quotation `(...) builds syntax with antiquotations. Elaborators (elab) go further, running arbitrary MetaM/TacticM code, with Expr as the AST for terms. The result is that Mathlib's DSLs, Aesop, and Clean's circuit syntax are ordinary libraries, not compiler features.
Block 5 — Trust, Axioms, and Independent Checking (6)

Q26. What does a "complete" Lean proof actually mean, concretely? (1) It elaborated without errors, (2) #print axioms thm lists only the accepted axioms (normally propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound) and no sorryAx, (3) the kernel accepted every declaration in the dependency closure, and (4) the statement means what you think it means. Items 1 to 3 are mechanical. Item 4 is the human-judgment gap (Q30). A proof that "compiles" while sorry appears only as a warning is not a proof.

Q27. What are native_decide and Lean.ofReduceBool, and what do they add to the trusted base? decide asks the kernel to evaluate a Decidable instance to isTrue. native_decide instead compiles and runs the instance with the code generator and asserts the result using the axiom Lean.ofReduceBool. This is orders of magnitude faster but moves the compiler, the evaluator, and the runtime into the trusted base: a miscompilation could "prove" a false statement. Reflective tactics (bv_decide, large computational proofs) take the same route. Careful projects therefore track whether a headline theorem depends on ofReduceBool, and some forbid it.

Q28. How can a Lean file "pass" while proving something unsound? Lean is extensible: metaprograms can add declarations directly to the environment, unsafe/implemented_by change runtime behaviour, set_option flags can disable checks, a bad axiom can be declared, and #exit or errors masked by tooling can truncate checking. A green build in a CI log only certifies "no error reported" from the checker you ran in the environment you ran it. A real audit replays the exported declarations through a fresh, independent kernel with a fixed axiom allowlist, and confirms the theorem statement matches a trusted specification.

Q29. What independent checkers exist, and what does a second kernel actually protect against? Lean can export its environment, and other implementations re-check it: lean4lean (the kernel written in Lean), nanoda (Rust), and [2026] con-leche, which the Lean FRO describes as an external checker whose consistency is proven in Lean. A second implementation protects against implementation bugs in one kernel (a crash, a missed universe check, a bad reduction). It cannot protect against a flaw in the theory both implement or against a wrong statement. For the FLT artifact (Q48), the authors report a second-kernel check with nanoda, which is exactly this layered defense.

Q30. What is the "misformalization" problem, with concrete Lean examples? Lean's definitions have conventions that can silently change meaning: Nat subtraction truncates (3 - 5 = 0), division by zero is 0, Real.sqrt of a negative is 0, and List.head! panics rather than failing in logic. A theorem about a wrongly specified object is a true theorem about the wrong thing. This is the main residual risk of any formal verification, especially in security: a flawless proof of a spec that omits the attacker model proves nothing about attacks. Mitigations: state key theorems in plain, reviewable terms; add sanity theorems that the spec is non-trivial (satisfiable and not vacuously true); and use independent statement checkers (the "comparator" idea used for FLT).

Q31. How does Lean's design separate "search" from "checking," and why is that the central idea of the whole ecosystem? Every automated step, from simp and omega to external SAT solvers, CAS systems, and LLM agents, is untrusted and only needs to emit a certificate the kernel (or a small verified checker) accepts. Lean tactics for polynomial arithmetic increasingly follow the pattern "call a computer algebra system, then verify its certificate in Lean." Checking a certificate is cheap and trustworthy; finding one is hard and may be arbitrarily unreliable. This asymmetry is precisely the same structure as "prover/verifier" in ZK, and it's why AI-generated proofs (Block 9) are safe to consume at scale.
Block 6 — Lean as a Programming Language & Verified Software (5)

Q32. How does Lean compile and run programs efficiently with a pure functional semantics? Lean compiles to an intermediate representation and then to C (with an experimental LLVM path). Memory is managed by reference counting with borrow inference, and the compiler inserts explicit inc/dec operations (the "Counting Immutable Beans" design). Because many objects are uniquely referenced, "functional" updates can be done in place at runtime. The logical semantics stay pure. Compiler infrastructure is actively being modernized in the 2025 to 2026 timeframe, but the contract is stable: what the logic says and what the compiled code does should agree (except where unsafe/implemented_by is involved).

Q33. What is FBIP ("functional but in place") and why do Lean programs care? When an Array, String, or structure is uniquely referenced, operations like arr.set i v or structure update mutate the existing buffer instead of copying it. If the reference count is above 1, a copy happens first. So a loop that threads an array linearly gets imperative performance, while the same code with an accidental second reference becomes quadratic. Performance-sensitive Lean code is therefore written to preserve uniqueness (avoid retaining stale references), a discipline invisible in the logic but central in practice.

Q34. How does do-notation relate to the pure core? do blocks desugar into monadic binds. Mutable variables (let mut) become state threaded through the continuation, for loops become calls to forIn with a monadic body, and early return/break/continue are encoded by an extra continuation or exception-like layer. So imperative-looking code is still a pure term, which can be unfolded, simulated, and reasoned about. Verification frameworks exploit this: [2025–2026] Lean's monadic verification support (Std.Do with the mvcgen verification-condition generator) generates Hoare-style obligations from do programs, and CSLib's Boole intermediate language aims to bring ordinary imperative code into the same pipeline.

Q35. How does Aeneas turn Rust into something provable in Lean? Rust's ownership discipline means a safe program's memory behaviour is largely local, so a verifier can avoid a heap model. Charon extracts Rust's MIR into a borrow-aware IR, and Aeneas translates it into pure functional Lean. A function taking a mutable borrow is translated into a forward function (its result) plus a backward function (how the borrowed value is updated when the borrow ends). You then prove properties of the resulting Lean functions with ordinary tools (grind, omega, Mathlib). [2026] Microsoft Research reports using this route on production cryptographic code in SymCrypt, and the Rust-to-Lean pipeline for zkVM primitives (Q47) builds on the same stack.

Q36. What does AWS's use of Lean for Cedar illustrate about "verified-guided development"? Cedar (the authorization language behind Amazon Verified Permissions) has its core semantics and analyses formalized in Lean. The production implementation is in Rust, and the team differentially tests the Rust code against the Lean model continuously, relying on Lean's fast runtime. The pattern: use the proof assistant for the specification and key theorems (e.g., the validator is sound), then use the executable spec as a test oracle for the shipped code. This pragmatic split, proofs for the properties that matter and oracle-testing for the rest, is how most industrial Lean adoption works.
Block 7 — Mathlib & Large-Scale Formalization (5)

Q37. Why is Mathlib's algebraic hierarchy hard to design, and what breaks when it's wrong? Classes like Monoid → Group → Ring → Field form a lattice with diamonds (the same structure reachable by several paths). If two paths produce propositionally-but-not-definitionally equal instances, unification fails at instance transparency: rw can't match, simp can't fire, and elaboration slows. Mathlib therefore designs classes with bundled structure fields (extends) so that paths agree by unfolding, and runs linters to catch non-defeq diamonds. Typeclass-inference performance (Q15) is a first-order concern in every refactor.

Q38. How does a blueprint-driven formalization project work? A blueprint is a LaTeX dependency graph of the informal proof: each lemma gets a statement, an informal proof, and links to the Lean declaration that formalizes it, with status colors (stated, proved, mathlib-ready). Humans (and now AI agents) can pick any unproved leaf, formalize it independently, and the graph shows what remains. The Fermat's Last Theorem project (Imperial, led by Kevin Buzzard) used this approach to organize hundreds of contributors. [2026] Tooling such as LeanArchitect aims to generate blueprints automatically, and platforms like Prove2Me and Lean Atlas target human-AI collaboration at scale.

Q39. Why is maintaining a big formal library an engineering problem of its own? Every Lean release can change tactics, instances, or unification behaviour, and a 2M+ line library must be ported in lockstep. Mathlib handles this with deprecation aliases, automated migration scripts, benchmark bots (the FLoC 2026 slides track per-commit hardware-normalized build cost and flag toolchain releases with >2% impact), and a linter suite. [2026] The reported trend is encouraging: build cost down roughly 28% from its peak despite substantial growth, while a paper titled Growing Mathlib documents the maintenance practices.

Q40. What did the human FLT-in-Lean project aim to prove, and why did it matter before 2026? Buzzard's project (started 2024) aimed to reduce FLT to results established in the 1980s (before Wiles's full argument) and formalize that reduction, because the deepest ingredients (modularity lifting, Langlands–Tunnell, parts of Mazur's and Ribet's work) were not yet in Mathlib. It was expected to take years and drove Mathlib's development of schemes, Galois representations, Hecke algebras, and more. It also drove pre-2026 milestones such as the formalization of FLT for regular primes. This is the backdrop for Q48.

Q41. What other 2026 formalization milestones stress-test Lean in different ways? [2026] Reported at or around FLoC 2026: Lean formalization of Viazovska's sphere-packing proofs; a $1000 Erdős-prize problem resolved with a ChatGPT-assisted Lean proof (Alexeev & Mixon, "vibe proving"); Move's borrow checker formalized (Sergey's group); computational algebra with Hex, which includes a verified LLL algorithm and a demo of Coppersmith's attack. They exercise different parts of the system: pure mathematics (type-class and universe load), computation (verified algorithms run natively), and software semantics (large inductive definitions and proof automation).
Block 8 — Lean × ZK & Cryptography (6)

Q42. How is Schwartz–Zippel stated and used in Lean? Mathlib contains a formal statement of the lemma: for a nonzero multivariate polynomial of total degree d over a finite set S of field elements, the number of zeros in S^n is at most d · |S|^(n-1), i.e. probability ≤ d/|S|. In a formalized protocol proof this lemma is the base case for soundness (the same one-line argument as Q7 in the ZK set), consumed inside a probabilistic framework (VCV-io for oracle/game-based reasoning). The formal version forces details paper proofs elide: the sampling distribution, whether challenges are from a subset of the field, and where independence is assumed.

Q43. Why does verifying a circuit over BN254 force you to prove a 254-bit number is prime? Circuit soundness arguments use that the base field is a field: no zero divisors (so b * x = 0 ∧ x ≠ 0 → b = 0), inverses exist for nonzero elements. In Lean that means a Field (ZMod p) instance, which requires Fact (Nat.Prime p). For cryptographic p, norm_num-style primality won't terminate on trial division. The standard route is a Pratt certificate (a recursive proof of primality using a primitive root and the factorization of p-1), checked by reflection. The ZK/SEC post on verifying a Poseidon circuit in Clean noted, with some humor, that the last sorry was about primality. The lesson: the "boring" field assumptions that paper proofs wave through are real proof obligations.

Q44. How does Clean model a circuit, and how does its soundness theorem correspond to "not underconstrained"? [2026] Clean is an embedded Lean DSL for ZK circuits. A FormalCircuit bundles: main (the circuit, as constraints and witness generation), Spec (the high-level meaning, e.g. b = if x = 0 then 1 else 0), soundness (for every assignment satisfying the constraints, Spec holds, independent of how the prover chose witnesses), and completeness (the honest witness generator produces an assignment satisfying the constraints). The isZero gadget (b := 1 - x*z, b*x = 0) is the canonical example: soundness uses the field's no-zero-divisors property. An underconstrained circuit (Q49 in the ZK set) is exactly a circuit for which the soundness theorem is false, so a failed soundness proof is a bug report, and a successful one is a machine-checked proof that no malicious witness exists.

Q45. What did "Channels" add to Clean in 2026, and what does it mean for zkVMs? [2026] Real zkVMs aren't one circuit but many AIR tables (CPU, memory, ALU, hash) linked by lookups or buses. Clean's Channel primitive models those cross-table interactions: each table emits or consumes tuples on a channel, and global soundness reduces to a multiset-balance condition (everything emitted is consumed, with multiplicities). This moves verification from isolated gadgets to end-to-end multi-AIR systems (the June 2026 update). It formalizes the same logic that lookup arguments (the ZK set's Q29) rely on, but at the level of "the whole VM's semantics follow from every table's local spec plus channel balance."

Q46. What are ArkLib, VCV-io and CompPoly, and what is being proven with them? [2026] They're the Verified-zkEVM project's libraries (funded in part under the Ethereum Foundation's zkEVM verification effort): ArkLib formalizes arguments of knowledge (IOPs/interactive oracle reductions and the protocols built on them); VCV-io provides an oracle-computation framework for game-based cryptographic proofs (probabilistic programs with oracle access); CompPoly gives computable polynomials so protocol specs are executable as well as provable. Together they aim at the full chain: field and polynomial algebra, then protocol soundness (round-by-round or knowledge soundness), then implementation correctness. These formalizations are active research. Treat any specific protocol's status (e.g. FRI or WHIR family) as something to check in the repo, not assume.

Q47. What is the Rust-to-Lean pipeline for zkVM primitives, and where do AI provers fit? [2026] A May 2026 experience report describes: (1) extract production Rust (Plonky3, RISC Zero primitives) into Lean using Charon/Aeneas or Hax, (2) state correctness against mathematical specifications from ArkLib/CompPoly, (3) discharge the resulting proof obligations with AI provers (Harmonic's Aristotle, Logical Intelligence's Aleph) with humans supervising. The key insight is the Q31 asymmetry: the AI is untrusted and the kernel is not, so every closed obligation is genuinely checked. Complementary 2026 work includes a formal verification of the s-two (Stwo) AIR, Nethermind's Halo2-in-Lean line of work, and zkVM testing tools. Real-world motivation: bugs such as the RISC-V instruction issues patched in RISC Zero 2.1.0 are precisely the soundness gaps this approach targets.
Block 9 — AI × Lean (3)

Q48. What exactly did Anthropic's Fermat's Last Theorem formalization establish, and what does it not? [2026] On 4 Sep 2026 Anthropic published a complete Lean 4 formalization of FLT (for positive integers and n ≥ 3, aⁿ + bⁿ ≠ cⁿ), generated by many Claude agents working largely autonomously for ~11 days: ~13M lines (about five times Mathlib), ~30,300 theorems proved with ~29,500 used. It contains no sorry, depends only on propext, Classical.choice, and Quot.sound, was re-checked by a second kernel (nanoda), and a comparator confirmed the proved statement matches Mathlib's own FLT statement. What it doesn't do: discover new mathematics. It formalizes the existing Wiles/Taylor–Wiles line of argument, and Kevin Buzzard, who led the human effort, confirmed it checks out while saying it "tells us essentially nothing" new mathematically; its significance is demonstrating autoformalization at scale. The residual trust is the layered boundary from Q26 to Q30: the kernel, Mathlib's definitions, the toolchain, and that the statement says what we think.

Q49. Why is Lean the default substrate for AI theorem provers, mechanically? Because it supplies a cheap, exact, unforgeable reward signal: a proof attempt either type-checks or it doesn't, with no human judgment, which suits reinforcement learning and best-of-N search. Add a huge corpus (Mathlib), a programmatic interface (REPL/servers such as Kimina's Lean server for RL pipelines), and the Q31 property (untrusted generator, trusted checker). The lineage: AlphaProof (DeepMind), Seed-Prover (ByteDance), DeepSeek-Prover-V2, Goedel-Prover-V2 (Princeton), Kimina-Prover, BFS-Prover, Aristotle (Harmonic). [2026] Newer agentic designs (e.g. MerLean-Prover with planning/check/Lean agents) report progress on PhD-qualifying-exam-style benchmarks. Autoformalization is also moving to whole textbooks and literature (several 2026 arXiv efforts) and to physics (PhysLib).

Q50. If AI can now produce proofs at scale, where does the remaining difficulty move? To specifications and statements (Q30). Checking is solved at the level of "does this term type-check"; the open problems are whether the definitions are faithful, whether the theorem is the right one, and whether the trust boundary (axioms, ofReduceBool, toolchain, environment replay) is acceptable for the application. Expect 2026-onward work on reviewing and auditing statements (sanity lemmas, independent comparators, "Lean-as-judge" evaluation of natural-language reasoning), on keeping giant AI-generated libraries maintainable (Q39), and on making human review of definitions cheap. For ZK specifically, it means the bottleneck shifts from "can we prove this circuit correct" to "is the circuit's Spec the property the protocol needs, including the attacker model." A proof is only as good as the question it answers.
Closing note

Nearly everything above reduces to three moves, reused constantly: (1) shrink trust to a tiny kernel and make everything else produce certificates it can check (de Bruijn criterion: tactics, SAT solvers, CAS, and AI agents are all just proposers); (2) treat computation as proof (definitional equality, reflection, decide, verified checkers executed at native speed, at the price of explicit axioms like ofReduceBool); and (3) put the risk where the machine can't help: in the statement. If you can point at any mechanism in this list and say which of those three moves it is, you've understood it at the level these questions are testing for.
